You must be done dealing with distractions and phone addiction by now.

Maybe someone has told you that you're addicted to your phone. Or maybe your parents have taunted you for being lazy because of how much time you spend on it.

(Well, no matter what, Indian parents love to blame phones for everything. That's another story.)

And you've probably tried to reduce your distractions by uninstalling apps and turning off notifications. Maybe you've watched countless YouTube videos about improving focus, only to receive the same advice: keep your phone away, uninstall distracting apps, and develop better habits.

None of this is wrong. These strategies can genuinely help.

But what if they're missing something?

Have you ever wondered why, despite trying so hard, you still find it difficult to put your phone down? What if you don't even want to use it, yet somehow keep reaching for it?

We often assume that excessive phone use is a sign of poor discipline. But sometimes, we're not reaching for our phones simply because we want entertainment. We might be trying to avoid something else.

## We See the Phone, Not the Problem

Imagine you've sat down to start an assignment, but you have no idea how to approach it. You feel confused, perhaps a little overwhelmed, so you pick up your phone to look something up. A few minutes later, you're scrolling through Instagram Reels.

Or perhaps you're feeling lonely, stressed, or bored. Instead of sitting with those feelings, you open YouTube and watch one video after another.

Are these behaviours necessarily signs of addiction or laziness?

Not always.

The behaviour looks similar in each situation, but the reasons behind it can differ. One person might simply want entertainment. Another might be avoiding a difficult task, while someone else might be looking for relief from an uncomfortable emotion.

From the outside, we see someone using their phone. We don't see what happened before they picked it up.

Sometimes, using our phones feels easier than facing a task we don't understand, a problem we don't know how to solve, or a feeling we would rather not experience.

This is what we can call emotional escape: turning our attention towards something else to get temporary relief from emotional discomfort.

And our phones make this remarkably easy. There's always another video to watch, another post to scroll through, or another game to play.

But why do we keep returning to the same distraction, even when we know it isn't helping us deal with the original problem?

## The Relief That Keeps Us Coming Back

Consider the student who avoids an assignment because they don't understand the topic. Whenever they think about starting, they feel overwhelmed. They pick up their phone, watch a few videos, and temporarily stop thinking about the assignment.

The assignment hasn't disappeared, but the discomfort has momentarily moved out of their attention.

That relief is real, and that's exactly why it works. The brain notices that something helped, and next time the same difficulty shows up, the phone feels like the obvious answer.

Psychologists have studied this pattern in procrastination. Fuschia Sirois and Timothy Pychyl describe it as short-term mood repair winning over long-term goals. In other words, procrastination isn't always a time-management problem. Sometimes, we're trying to manage a feeling in the short term, even when doing so makes things harder for us later.

The same pattern can appear in phone use. A 2026 study from the University of Essex found that people who strongly dislike boredom use their phones more, based on both their own reports and tracked screen time. Another study of 261 college students found that those who were less able to tolerate emotional distress were more likely to have problems with their phone use a month later.

And here's the part that stings: using our phones to avoid uncomfortable feelings doesn't necessarily make those feelings disappear. A Ghent University study published in the journal Emotion examined the relationship between phone use for emotion regulation and people's feelings afterwards. Its findings suggest that using a phone to manage emotions doesn't necessarily provide the lasting relief we might expect.

So the assignment stays incomplete, the deadline gets closer, and the pressure increases. We feel even more tempted to escape, and the pattern continues.

This doesn't mean every instance of phone use follows this cycle. Taking a break, watching videos, or talking to friends can be perfectly reasonable. The concern arises when we repeatedly use our phones to avoid things we need or want to address, especially when we struggle to change that pattern.

## When We Blame Laziness, What Do We Miss?

We often respond to excessive phone use by focusing on discipline and time management. We make schedules, uninstall apps, and promise ourselves that tomorrow will be different.

Sometimes, these changes work. But they may not be enough when the behaviour is also driven by an underlying difficulty.

Imagine a student who avoids an assignment because they don't understand the topic. Even if they put their phone away, the confusion remains. The task is still difficult, and so is the feeling that made them want to avoid it in the first place.

Similarly, someone who turns to their phone whenever they feel lonely may spend less time on it, but that doesn't necessarily mean the loneliness disappears.

In both situations, the phone use is visible, but what lies behind it may not be.

Controlling a behaviour and understanding it are two different things. Yet, when our attempts to control it fail, we often blame ourselves for lacking discipline instead of questioning whether there is more to the behaviour than we initially assumed.

## But Is Emotional Escape Always the Reason?

Of course, we need to be careful not to replace one assumption with another.

Not everyone who spends hours on their phone is trying to escape uncomfortable emotions. Sometimes, phone use is just a habit. Sometimes, people simply enjoy the entertainment, social connections, and communities they find online. App designs that encourage continued engagement also make it harder to stop.

These reasons can overlap, too. Someone might open an app out of boredom, continue scrolling out of habit, and eventually realise they've spent much longer on it than intended.

We cannot look at someone's screen time and conclude that we know what they're feeling or why they're behaving that way.

Understanding the reasons behind our behaviour also doesn't mean avoiding responsibility for changing it. We can recognise that a task makes us anxious while still taking steps to complete it. We can acknowledge that scrolling has become a habit while working to change that habit.

Understanding isn't an excuse for the behaviour. It's where changing it begins.

## Ask a Different Question

Think about the last time you reached for your phone when you had something else to do. What was happening just before you picked it up?

Were you bored? Were you avoiding a task that felt difficult? Were you trying not to think about something that was bothering you?

You won't always find an answer. But the question changes how you see the behaviour. What looked like a lack of discipline may turn out to be a reaction to something you hadn't noticed.

There isn't one reason behind everyone's phone use, so there can't be one solution for everyone. Perhaps that's what we overlook when we label excessive phone use as laziness. We focus on the behaviour we can see without considering what's driving it.

So instead of asking only, "Why can't I put my phone down?", perhaps we should also ask:

"What makes me want to pick it up in the first place?"

Because sometimes, changing our relationship with our phones begins with understanding not just how much we use them, but what we're looking for when we reach for them.
