Has it ever happened to you that you asked a friend what they were doing, only to hear that they were so busy they couldn't even talk?

At first, it seems like an ordinary conversation. Your friend has things to do, responsibilities to handle, and goals to work toward. There's nothing wrong with that.

But somewhere in that conversation, you start questioning yourself. What are you doing with your time? Are you working hard enough? Are you learning enough? While your friend seems occupied with something important, you suddenly wonder whether you're doing enough with your own life.

The strange part is that your friend hasn't said anything about your life. They haven't called you lazy or told you that you're wasting your time. Yet, their busyness has made you question your own productivity.

Why?

Why does someone else's packed schedule become a reason to doubt our own progress? And why does being busy feel like an achievement, even when we know nothing about what that person is actually accomplishing?

Perhaps it's because we've learned to associate busyness with ambition. We call someone hardworking simply because they're occupied. Working late nights, skipping breaks, and having a packed schedule are often seen as signs of dedication. Meanwhile, someone with free time may be seen as lazy, even when they've finished everything they needed to do. Being busy has become a way to appear successful.

But being busy only means being occupied. It tells us nothing about what we're actually achieving or whether we're getting closer to our goals.

Take me, for example. I can sit at my desk for two hours with a pen and paper and still end up with a completely blank page. Sometimes, I spend an entire day looking for a topic to write about, only to end the day with no topic and a lot of frustration. At this point, I've spent hours working, but the only thing I've successfully achieved is sitting at my desk for hours!

Yet, from the outside, I might look like someone who's working really hard. And that's the problem. We often judge productivity by what we can see: how busy someone looks. We rarely know what their efforts are actually achieving. The problem begins when we use that same standard to judge ourselves.

Imagine you've completed your day's study targets and finally have some free time. Then you find out that another student is learning an extra skill, attending a course, or working on another project. Suddenly, the work you've done doesn't feel like enough. You start wondering whether you should be doing more, too.

Maybe you genuinely want to learn that skill. Or maybe you're afraid that if you don't, you'll fall behind.

That difference matters.

When the fear of falling behind takes over, we start adding tasks to our schedules, not because they serve a purpose, but because having free time makes us uncomfortable. We pick up extra work and keep looking for something else to do. We tell ourselves we're being ambitious, but sometimes we're just trying to escape the feeling that everyone else is moving forward while we're standing still. Somehow, we've created a kind of artificial busyness.

Instead of asking whether a task is useful, we start asking whether it makes us feel productive. Our goal slowly shifts from making progress to proving, to ourselves and sometimes to others, that we're not falling behind.

But this never really ends, because there will always be someone doing more. There will always be another skill to learn, another project to start, or another person whose schedule makes ours look inadequate. If we keep measuring ourselves against what others are doing, the standard keeps changing, and no matter how much we do, it may still feel like we're not doing enough.

This doesn't mean that learning new skills, working hard, or taking on responsibilities is pointless. These things can help us grow. What matters is why we're doing them. Are we doing them because they matter to us, or because someone else's schedule has made us doubt ourselves?

Having free time shouldn't automatically be treated as a problem, either. One person may be handling urgent responsibilities, while another has finished their work and has time to spare. Their schedules are different, but that alone doesn't tell us who is making more meaningful progress. Managing our time should be about making room for what matters, not making every hour look productive.

So, the next time you feel the urge to add something to your schedule, pause for a moment. Would you still do it if no one knew about it? Sometimes, the answer might help you understand whether you're working toward something you want or simply trying to convince yourself that you're not falling behind.
