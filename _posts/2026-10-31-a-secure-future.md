*“Do you know Sharma Ji's son got a government job? They were so happy! They've invited us to their home for the celebration. Now see how people will respect them. He'll even get better marriage proposals. And look at you. You're just wasting your time on such a useless course.*

*Why don't you start preparing for government exams? Maybe you won't clear it on your first attempt, but if you keep trying, you'll eventually get a job.”*

*“But Dad, I want to do something else.”*

*“I don't know anything. Just start preparing for government exams. I've invested so much money in your education. Can't I expect this much from you? Don't you want me to be able to walk with respect in society?”*

A conversation like this might sound familiar to many young people in India.

In India, many families give government jobs a special place when it comes to choosing a career. And honestly, there is nothing wrong with that. A government job can offer several benefits, such as a stable income, job security, social respect, and, depending on the position and service rules, retirement benefits.

Parents who have faced financial struggles often want their children to have a more secure life than they had. They don't want them to face the same uncertainty. For them, a government job can seem like one of the safest ways to build a stable future.

There is also a social side to it. In many families and communities, a government job brings respect and can even influence how a person is viewed when it comes to marriage. All these things have made a government job more than just a career choice for many families.

But what happens when the desire to give a child a secure future turns into an expectation that the child cannot question?

There is no doubt that many young people genuinely want to work in the government sector. They prepare for exams such as UPSC, SSC, banking, and other recruitment examinations because it is the career they want. Some also choose this path after considering their other options. There is nothing wrong with that.

But what about those who have never really explored what they want to do? Those who are interested in something else but are expected to prepare for government exams because their parents believe it is the best option?

This is where a preference can slowly turn into pressure.

A child may be expected to fulfill their parents' expectations without being given enough space to talk about what they actually want. They may not get the chance to explore different career options or discuss the benefits and risks of the path they want to choose. Instead, they are simply expected to prepare for an exam, sometimes without even knowing whether they genuinely want the job they are preparing for.

And saying, “I don't want to do this,” isn't always easy. Some young people may fear disappointing their parents or being seen as disrespectful or ungrateful. So, instead of discussing what they want, they may stay silent and continue on a path they aren't sure about.

The pressure doesn't end once the preparation begins.

Government exams are highly competitive, and getting selected is not guaranteed. Yet, some young people are told to keep giving attempts, even when they no longer want to continue or want to explore other options. Stopping can begin to feel like failure, especially after years of preparation.

Then comes comparison.

A relative's child clears an exam. A neighbor gets a government job. Their achievements become examples that a young person is expected to follow. Over time, these comparisons can make them feel like they aren't doing enough or that they are falling behind.

But this raises an important question: **Is a government job the only definition of success?**

Different people have different interests, abilities, priorities, and goals. Some genuinely want a government job, while others may want to explore a different field and find what suits them. There are many career options, and each comes with its own opportunities, challenges, and risks.

But having different options doesn't mean every career is suitable for everyone or that every choice will guarantee success.

Of course, financial security matters. Choosing a career also means understanding its opportunities, risks, and practical realities. But is security the only factor that should decide someone's career? And how can one career be the right choice for everyone?

A government job can be a great career choice, but that doesn't mean every other career is inferior or irresponsible. Someone who chooses a different path may simply have different interests, abilities, and goals.

This doesn't mean parents should stop guiding their children. Their experience and concerns about financial security are important. But guidance should leave room for discussion.

Parents can help their children understand different career options, discuss the risks and opportunities, and make more informed decisions. At the same time, young people also need to take responsibility for their choices and understand that every career comes with its own challenges.

The problem is not that parents want a secure and respectable future for their children. The problem begins when they assume that there is only one way to achieve it.
